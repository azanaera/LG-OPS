# How a Shipment Works, in Plain English

For anyone who has not worked in logistics. No prior knowledge needed. The example route is made up for illustration.

## The one-sentence version

A freight forwarder is a travel agent for cargo. The company arranges for goods to get from a seller in one country to a buyer in another, using ships, planes, trucks and customs officers it does not own. It makes money on the gap between what it charges the customer and what it pays those suppliers.

## A story to hold onto

A furniture maker in Manila wants to send 40 chairs to a shop in Dubai.

1. **They ask for a price.** The maker emails us: what would it cost, and how long would it take? We work out the cost from rate tables: the shipping line's charge, trucking, paperwork fees and our own margin. We send a **quote**.
2. **They say yes.** We open a **file**, which is one folder for this one shipment. Everything about the job hangs off it, and it gets a number so nobody mixes it up with another job.
3. **They give us the details.** We need an invoice for the chairs, a list of what is in each box, and who is sending and receiving. This is the paperwork that says what the goods are and what they are worth.
4. **We book space.** The chairs have to travel on someone's ship or plane, so we reserve room with the shipping line or airline. They confirm and give us a booking reference.
5. **A manager checks the file.** Before money or movement is set in motion, a second person looks for mistakes. They can approve it or send it back with a note saying what to fix.
6. **We collect the chairs.** A truck picks them up from the maker's factory and brings them to our warehouse. Staff check that what arrived matches the paperwork: the count, the weight and the size of the boxes. Price depends on weight and space, so this step matters.
7. **We prepare the shipping document.** This is the receipt and contract for the journey. The maker checks that it is right and approves it. Ocean cargo gets a *bill of lading*. Air cargo gets an *air waybill*.
8. **Customs lets the goods leave.** A customs broker tells the government what is leaving and gets permission. Without it, the goods stay put.
9. **The ship or plane leaves.** The carrier takes the cargo and issues its own master document. We warn our partner in Dubai that the shipment is on its way (the **pre-alert**).
10. **The goods arrive.** Our partner in Dubai clears them through customs there, tells the shop the cargo has landed (the **arrival notice**), and delivers it. The shop signs to say it was received. That signature is the **proof of delivery**.
11. **We send the bill and close the books.** We invoice the customer and pay our own suppliers. At the end of the month, we lock the accounting records for good.

## Two words you will hear a lot

- **Ocean and air.** Ship cargo is slower and cheaper, and it travels in containers. Air cargo is faster and pricier, and it is charged by weight or by the space it takes up, whichever is bigger.
- **Consolidation.** Sometimes one airplane booking is shared by many small senders, like a carpool for cargo. Each sender gets their own document (a "house" one), and the airline sees one big booking under a single "master" document.

## Who does what

| Who | In plain terms |
|---|---|
| Shipper | The one sending the goods |
| Consignee | The one receiving them |
| Sales / Customer service | Prices the job and keeps the customer informed |
| Operations | Books the space and runs the shipment day to day |
| Warehouse / Trucker | Collects, stores and moves the goods physically |
| Documentation | Prepares the shipping papers |
| Customs broker | Gets government permission to send goods out or bring them in |
| Carrier / Airline | The ship or plane company that carries the cargo |
| Destination agent | Our partner at the other end |
| Finance | Bills customers, pays suppliers and keeps the accounts |

## The money, in three steps

1. We charge the customer. That list of charges is the **invoice**.
2. We pay the shipping line, the truckers and the agents. These are our **costs**.
3. Everything that touches the money is recorded in the company's books through a single checkpoint (the **accounting bridge**). Once recorded, an entry is never edited. If it was wrong, a new entry cancels it. At month-end the books for that month are locked.
